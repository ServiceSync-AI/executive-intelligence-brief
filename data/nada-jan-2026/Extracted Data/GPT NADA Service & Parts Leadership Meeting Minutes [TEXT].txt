Section 1: Meeting Priorities & Hot Topics

1. Responding to Calls & Customer Communication (01)
Core Issue:
We are not answering phones or responding to customers fast enough in service.
Key Challenges:
	•	BDC is not functioning effectively.
	•	Centrally located office causes calls to bounce between BDC and dealership.
	•	Customers experience delays and frustration.
	•	Phone systems are outdated and inefficient.
Opportunities & Solutions:
	•	Implement AI-driven phone trees (e.g., PAM AI).
	•	Focus on managing the majority use cases, not edge cases.
	•	AI allows rapid iteration and change (examples discussed from development boards).
	•	Shift from outbound calling to text-first communication.
	•	Vendors discussed: Matador, Toma.
	•	Missed-call recovery:
	•	System should auto-call back, take messages, and surface them on a dashboard.
	•	Allow multiple people in the department to respond.
	•	Emphasis on dealership-level customization.
	•	CSI is not currently a major concern:
	•	More positive than negative.
	•	Integration considerations:
	•	Tekion outreach and direct customer communication.

2. Service & Manufacturer Retention (02)
Service Retention:
	•	Industry focus highlighted (Forbes reference).
	•	Retention is where long-term profitability is built.
Manufacturer Retention (GM Example):
	•	First-year retention typically ranges 70–85%.
	•	Goal should be as close to 90% as possible.
	•	AI and proactive communication are critical levers to achieve this.
Key Metric Reminder:
	•	1396: The real money is in getting the customer back into the dealership.

3. Scheduling & Shop Load Balance (05 / 06)
General Observations:
	•	Scheduling is not always the problem—capacity and talent often are.
	•	Common realities:
	•	Too much work some days, not enough others.
	•	Cars sitting for days or weeks.
	•	Large jobs become irrelevant when technician count and skill level are low.
Advisor vs. System Perspective:
	•	Advisors think differently than schedulers.
	•	Advisors juggle multiple moving parts that scheduling systems don’t see.
Key Challenges:
	•	Limited time slots.
	•	Difficulty balancing:
	•	Appointment scheduling
	•	Actual shop load and technician availability
Discussion Points:
	•	Bring vehicles in based on true availability, not just appointment volume.
	•	Better alignment between scheduling and dispatch.

4. Video MPI Adoption & Culture (04 / 17)
Current State:
	•	Most stores want to do Video MPIs but are not executing consistently.
Barriers:
	•	Cultural resistance.
	•	Tech skepticism.
	•	Fear of added workload.
Best Practices Discussed:
	•	Video quality matters:
	•	No fly-by videos.
	•	Use both video and still photos.
	•	MPI quality directly increases sales.
	•	Performance accountability:
	•	Example discussed: If a tech completes under 80% of required videos, they are paid 80% of potential compensation.
	•	Implementation strategies:
	•	Start in Quick Lube, not the main shop.
	•	Be mindful of social pressure and perception.
	•	Staffing ideas:
	•	Hire college students to assist with video MPIs.

5. Technician Performance: Efficiency vs. Productivity (20)

	•	Some respond to pressure.

	•	Some require protection and support.
	•	Effective management comes from time spent knowing your people.
	•	Ties back to MPI execution and accountability.

Section 3: Execution, Efficiency & Fixed Ops Alignment
(Final Day Takeaways, Group Discussion & Composite Session 2)

1. The North Star: Hours per RO & Proficiency
Primary Focus:
	•	Increase Hours per RO across:
	•	Main shop
	•	Quick lube
	•	Fill available technician hours every day.
Key Metric: Proficiency Rating
Hours Billed ÷ Hours Available
	•	Applies to every technician, including lube techs.
	•	Use the same calculation every time to establish a true benchmark.
	•	Core question:
	•	How is each technician filling an 8-hour day?
Reality Check:
	•	If a technician is sitting, we have failed the system.

2. Quick Lube: Separate, Measure, Grow
Key Takeaways:
	•	Quick lube must be separate from main service, not blended.
	•	Measure:
	•	Hours per RO (Quick Lube)
	•	Labor gross %
	•	Upsells in the drive
	•	Flat-rate structures and clear expectations outperform hourly ambiguity.
Action Areas:
	•	Increase upsells in quick lube.
	•	Use quick lube as a feeder into main shop.
	•	Track:
	•	Quick lube hours per RO
	•	Service gross per technician

3. MPI Discipline: Video, Quality & Daily Focus
Non-Negotiables:
	•	Implement quality video MPI.
	•	Focus on:
	•	Clear explanations
	•	Photos + video
	•	No “fly-by” inspections
Why It Matters:
	•	Direct impact on:
	•	Hours per RO
	•	Customer trust
	•	Advisor confidence
Daily Execution:
	•	Daily huddle:
	•	Align team on hour targets
	•	Identify gaps early
	•	Set objectives for the day

4. Declines, TOs & Manager Involvement
Key Principles:
	•	Declined service = manager opportunity, not lost cause.
	•	Formal TO process in service must exist—just like sales.
Best Practices:
	•	Manager TO on:
	•	Large declines
	•	Safety items
	•	Extended warranty confusion
	•	Increase collections from extended warranties.
	•	Change parts handling for extended warranty work to speed approvals and billing.

5. Sales → Service Handoff & First-Visit Retention
Critical Insight:
	•	The first service visit determines lifetime retention.
Action Items:
	•	Improve sales-to-service handoff.
	•	Why not introduce the customer to service before they leave sales?
	•	Pre-call online appointments:
	•	Confirm
	•	Set expectations
	•	Ask if customer wants to add anything

6. Central Dispatch, Shop Foreman & Flow Control
Group Consensus:
	•	Keep advisors out of the shop.
	•	One point of contact:
	•	Shop foreman
	•	Central dispatch

Dispatch Models:

9. Parts: The Hidden Driver of Proficiency
9.1 Parts Preloading & Runners
What Works:
	•	Parts runners delivering to stalls.
	•	Pre-pulling parts for the day.
Common Objection:
	•	“Parts get lost on the counter.”
Reality:
	•	Poor process, not bad concept.
	•	Preloading parts is one of the biggest proficiency gains available.

9.2 Special Order Parts = Dead Money Until Installed
Problems Identified:
	•	Special order parts sit uninstalled.
	•	Appointments not set when parts arrive.
	•	Inconsistent follow-up.
Best Practices:
	•	Review special order parts twice weekly (or daily via dashboard).
	•	Track:
	•	Date ordered
	•	Date arrived
	•	Appointment set
	•	CDK “Blue Sheet” process:
	•	Parts manager flags arrival
	•	Advisor + BDC notified to schedule
	•	Key insight:
	•	Special order parts are dead dollars unless prepaid or installed.
Opportunity:
	•	AI-driven special order parts follow-up:
	•	Texting
	•	Calling
	•	Appointment setting

9.3 Lost Sales: Measure Demand, Not Gut
Definition:
	•	Lost sale = customer needed a part and went elsewhere.
Key Metric:
	•	Record lost sales as Hits, not failures.
Critical Question:
	•	What do we do with this data?
What Should Happen:
	•	Identify true demand.
	•	Adjust stocking.
	•	Align parts and service managers weekly.
	•	Seasonal adjustments matter.
Reminder:
	•	It’s math.
	•	Gut decisions get dealers in trouble.
